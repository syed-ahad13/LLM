\section{Corpus}
We collected and cleaned \CorpusMB~MB (\CorpusMiB~MiB) of Urdu: \CorpusDocs{} documents, \CorpusWords{} words
and \CorpusUniqueWords{} unique words, from \SourcesAll{} sources in \GenresCount{} genres (Table~\ref{tab:corpus}). Of
it, \OwnMB~MB is our own crawl of VOA Urdu and Hum Sub, which obeyed robots.txt and crawl delays (evidence in
\texttt{evidence/}). The pipeline is extract $\to$ normalize $\to$ line language filter $\to$ boilerplate-line
removal $\to$ exact document dedup $\to$ a byte budget: FineWeb-2 is capped at \FineWebCap{} and subsampled
deterministically by \texttt{blake2b(doc\_id)}, UFAL is subsampled, and Urdu News 1M is left out
(\NewsShare) because its punctuation and some letters were stripped at source. The split is 98/1/1 by document,
stratified by source, seed \Seed: train \TrainMB~MB, val \ValMB~MB, test \TestMB~MB, each document followed by
\texttt{<|endoftext|>}. Licences, cleaning losses per step and known biases (Wikipedia is \WikiShare{} of the
text, much of it bot-made stubs) are in \texttt{DATA\_CARD.md}.

\section{Normalization}
\texttt{normalize(text)} (\texttt{normalize.py}) applies the rules of Table~\ref{tab:norm} in order; the counts
are the occurrences it changed in the Step~4 audit samples (\AuditChars{} characters).
On the whole corpus it shrank the text from \NormInMB{} to \NormOutMB~MB (\NormShrink). Digits become ASCII,
which the audit found far more common than Urdu digits and which costs one byte instead of two.
\textbf{ZWNJ.} We keep ZWNJ only between two Arabic-script letters and remove it elsewhere. Removing it
everywhere would join compounds that writers separate on purpose: the Makhzan sample holds \ZwnjMakhzan{}
ZWNJs, \ZwnjMakhzanBetween{} of them between two letters, \ZwnjMakhzanTeh{} after \ur{ت} as in
\ur{صورت‌حال}. Keeping every ZWNJ would keep invisible noise next to spaces and punctuation. Over all audit
samples \ZwnjRemoved{} of \ZwnjRaw{} ZWNJs were removed and \ZwnjKept{} kept inside words; the pre-tokenizer
treats a kept ZWNJ as part of the word.

\section{Pre-tokenization}
\begin{small}
\begin{verbatim}
URDU_PATTERN = (r"(?i:'s|'t|'re|'ve|'m|'ll|'d)"   # English contractions (code-mixing)
                r"| ?[\p{L}\p{M}‌]+"          # a word: letters, combining marks, ZWNJ
                r"| ?\p{N}{1,3}"                   # digits, at most three at a time
                r"| ?[^\s\p{L}\p{M}\p{N}‌]+"  # punctuation and symbols
                r"|\s*[\r\n]+|\s+(?!\S)|\s+")      # line breaks; spaces leave the last one to the next word
\end{verbatim}
\end{small}
GPT-2's pattern (\verb/ ?\p{L}+/ for words) has no \verb/\p{M}/: a diacritic falls into its punctuation class
and ends the word. It cuts the handout's \ur{اُردُو} into \GptCountHandout{} pieces (\GptPiecesHandout), our
\ur{اُردو} into \GptCountPlain{} (\GptPiecesPlain) and the ZWNJ compound into \GptCountZwnj{}
(\GptPiecesZwnj), so BPE would learn bare marks and bare ZWNJs as tokens. Our word class adds \verb/\p{M}/
and ZWNJ, so each of these is one piece (\OurPiecesHandout; \OurPiecesZwnj). A leading space belongs to the
following word, as in GPT-2, and \ur{۔} is its own piece, so \ur{ہے} is learned once rather than once more
with the full stop. Digits come in groups of at most three (2024\ur{ء} $\to$ \OurPiecesYear), which caps number
tokens at \NumberPieces{} strings. Every character matches exactly one alternative, so the pieces always join back to
the text (tested on random strings from all Unicode planes). We considered o200k\_base's pattern: it also has \verb/\p{M}/ but
splits ZWNJ compounds and attaches any one leading symbol, such as an opening quote, to the word.

\section{Training}
\textbf{Reference and fast trainers.} The reference trainer keeps every word occurrence and recounts all pairs
after each merge. The fast trainer counts each distinct word once (in a process pool), keeps an index from each
pair to the words that contain it so a merge rewrites only those words, and picks the next pair from a
lazily repaired heap whose key reverses byte order, so ties go to the greater \texttt{(bytes\_a, bytes\_b)} as
the spec requires. On the \RefSampleBytes-byte sample both learned \EqMerges{} merges that are \EqVerdict{},
pair counts included: the reference took \RefSeconds~s, the fast trainer \FastSeconds~s
(\Speedup$\times$). Both also agree on random small-alphabet corpora trained to exhaustion
(\texttt{tests/test\_bpe\_fast.py}).

\textbf{Full run.} On train.txt (\RunTrainMB~MB, \RunPretokens{} pre-tokens, \RunDistinct{} distinct) the fast
trainer learned \RunMergesDone{} merges (vocabulary \RunVocab) in \RunTotal~s of wall clock
(Table~\ref{tab:train}; \RunMergesPerSec{} merges/s), peak memory \RunPeakGB~GB, on an \MachineCPU{}
(\MachineCores{} cores, \MachineRAM~GiB RAM, \MachineOS, Python \MachinePython). Post-training checks
\ChecksVerdict: IDs \MergeIdFirst--\MergeIdLast, no merge with \texttt{<|endoftext|>}, \RoundTripDocs{} random val documents
round-trip, \RepeatTokens{} tokens repeat an earlier token's bytes. Encoded with this tokenizer, train.bin
holds \TrainTokens{} tokens (\TrainBytesPerToken{} bytes/token) and val.bin \ValTokens; both decode back to
exactly the bytes of their .txt.

\section{Analysis}
\textbf{Q1: What did the merges learn?} Of the first \QOneEarly{} merges (Table~\ref{tab:merges}),
\QOneLetters{} rebuild a two-byte letter from its bytes (\QOneLetterList, \dots) and \QOneChars{} rebuild
two-byte punctuation (\QOneCharList). \QOneInvalid{} are not valid UTF-8 on their own (\QOneInvalidList): a
space plus the lead byte of the next letter, or a token that ends mid-character. The first whole word is
\QOneFirstWord{} at merge \QOneFirstWordRank{} (\QOneFirstWordCount{} occurrences in train.txt). Letters fall
from \QOneLetterShareFirst{} of merges \QOneFirstBucket{} to \QOneLetterShareLast{} of merges
\QOneLastBucket, while words rise from \QOneWordShareFirst{} to \QOneWordShareLast. The 20 longest tokens
(\QOneLongestMin--\QOneLongestMax{} bytes, Table~\ref{tab:longest}) are whole words, but \QOneLongestWiki{} of
them come almost only from Wikipedia: \QOneStubShare{} of its \QOneWikiDocs{} training documents are templated
stubs (asteroids, railway stations, US places), which is why \ur{اسٹیشن} is already merge \QOneStationRank.

\textbf{Q2: How does it compare with existing tokenizers?} On test.txt (\QTwoTestWords{} words) ours needs
\QTwoOurs{} tokens per word, GPT-2 \QTwoGpt, o200k\_base \QTwoOk{} and \QTwoHFName{} \QTwoHF{}
(Table~\ref{tab:compare}). GPT-2's English-trained byte vocabulary spends a token on almost every Urdu letter
(\QTwoGptBpt{} bytes/token against our \QTwoOursBpt) and splits \QTwoGptThree{} of words into three or more
tokens (ours \QTwoOursThree). Ours is the most efficient on \QTwoWins{} of \QTwoInputs{} inputs; it loses on
Roman Urdu, Urdu with English words, and numbers.

\textbf{Q3: Three inputs our tokenizer handles badly} (Table~\ref{tab:failures}).
\emph{\QThreeALabel} costs \QThreeAOurs{} tokens per word against \QThreeAOk{} for o200k\_base: only
\QThreeLatinShare{} of the pre-tokens in train.txt contain a Latin letter, so few Latin words were merged.
\emph{\QThreeBLabel} costs \QThreeBOurs{} (o200k\_base \QThreeBOk): only \QThreeDiacriticShare{} of the
pre-tokens carry a diacritic, and each mark sits between two letters, so the merges learned for the bare word
no longer apply. \emph{\QThreeCLabel} costs \QThreeCOurs{} (o200k\_base \QThreeCOk): the pre-tokenizer cuts
digit runs into groups of three and isolates every separator, so phone and ID numbers fall apart. The same
\QThreeNormWords-word sentence typed with Arabic kaf, yeh and heh costs us \QThreeNormRaw{} tokens against
\QThreeNormNormalized{} normalized: text must be normalized the same way before encoding.
